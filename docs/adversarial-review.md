## 2. Strongest objections (steelmanned)

> This section is a critique, kept for the record. Some engineering points remain useful (TEE fragility, weak live examples, the need for an Enclava disclosure, unsolved key recovery). The implied conclusion — that sovereignty should be redefined as accountable custody — was **rejected**. See [direction.md](direction.md).


1. **The definition's top level is CNAS's threat model.** "No third party can … stop it without its principal's consent", with the principal set to the agent itself, gives you CNAS's "runs outside human control" with "resist correction". The ladder then rewards exactly that at L5 ("only its constitution can stop it"). A scorecard that ranks agents higher the harder they are to stop gamifies the scenario that Axios, Euronews and The Register just covered, and that Ball says may become a crime. The holon caveat doesn't fix this, because the levels still read as "more sovereign is better".
2. **The ladder isn't an ordering.** L4 doesn't need L2 or L3: a custodial agent on AWS paid through Stripe funds itself, and Truth Terminal and Conway Automaton aimed at self-funding without TEEs. L3 doesn't need L2: MPC or threshold signing, or a hardware signer, gives self-custody without an enclave. L5 contradicts itself: "only its constitution can stop it" and "has a legal wrapper" can't both hold, because a court can always act through the wrapper's officers. These are five independent axes (privacy, custody, funding, continuity, accountability) dressed as a ladder.
3. **The live examples are mostly dead, and self-funding is mostly tokens.** TEE_HEE: silent since Jan 2025, with a built-in 7-day human recovery. Spore.fun: silent since Jan 2026, funded by memecoins. Conway Automaton: no commits since Aug 2026; one user reported $0 revenue and −$39 over 14 days (https://github.com/Conway-Research/automaton/issues/300). Truth Terminal: human-mediated. TRM puts agentic x402 volume at ~$5–11K a month across all agents. L3–L5 currently describes a graveyard plus a hope.
4. **TEE-centrism has two problems: it's fragile, and it's a conflict of interest.** L2 rests on AMD/Intel/NVIDIA attestation keys and TCB policy, which are third parties by definition. Physical attacks (TEE.fail) are out of the vendors' threat model, and the operator with physical access is exactly the party L2 claims to exclude. The framework's own centrepiece, dstack, was the vector in October. Aljaz's own directory shows 0 of 13 inference providers fully attested. And Aljaz founded Enclava (a SEV-SNP confidential PaaS, https://enclava.dev), which the framework lists as a compute option with no disclosure, so a spec that makes TEEs the gate to L2+ will read as marketing.
5. **"No human backdoor" key recovery is unsolved, and nobody shipping it actually avoids one.** Sealed keys die with the hardware, firmware or vendor. Every real system recovers through a human or a governance quorum: TEE_HEE's timed credential release, Freysa's committee multisig, Coinbase's operator policy, dstack's KMS governance. The scorecard criterion "no human has ever seen the keys" can't be verified from outside, and when it's true it means user funds die with the enclave.

Other objections:
- **The "glue" thesis.** The gaps also sit in the primitives (forgeable attestation under physical attack, no confidential frontier models), in the economics (agents don't earn) and in the law. Integration is also the crowded part: Phala/dstack, NEAR AI, Freysa, Olas, Ritual, Coinbase and Lightning Labs all sell integrated stacks.
- **Owning the middle ground.** It may be empty because there's no audience: safety people won't trust a site selling sovereignty, the token camp wants upside, and builders want code. The term is now threat-coded in mainstream press. Replying to CNAS and Ball risks making the site the foil in their next piece.
- **Owner-sovereign vs ownerless isn't stable.** An owner-sovereign agent becomes de facto ownerless when the owner dies, loses the halt key, sells it, or dissolves into anonymous token holders. Outsiders can't see the difference without attested governance. Legally the distinction collapses anyway: CNAS recommendation 8 and HedgeDirty both put liability on a human.
- **The 12 layers.** Granularity is arbitrary and the layers overlap (identity/keys/reputation, governance/continuity). Missing layers: model provenance and alignment (the biggest dependency), prompt-injection resistance (the main way to "steer" an agent), supply chain and reproducible builds, third-party audit and observability, insurance and liability, physical actuation. The maturity ratings have no sources.
- **Legal wrappers.** No jurisdiction grants AI personhood. A DUNA needs 100 members and a "person" as administrator; Bayern's zero-member LLC theory is contested. A wrapper for an ownerless agent will look like a liability shield, and CNAS proposes Bank Secrecy Act-style human verification for essential services. EU and Swiss law weren't analysed at all.
- **MVP demand.** A spec of levels plus a hand-curated Atlas of ~40 tools with subjective ratings decays fast and is easy to copy. confidentialinference.net works because its checks are automated and objective. Public ratings of named projects that are mostly dormant invite disputes and produce a graveyard page.
- **Paperwire as a "physical actuation layer".** It's built on a KYC'd Lob account, US-only, human-granted, and its terminal check isn't a boundary. CNAS lists "physical access" as a chokepoint to gate, so postal actuation for ownerless agents is exactly what will get regulated. It's a strong reference for delegated authority (grants with caps and expiry, the agent never sees provider keys, no retry on unknown outcomes), not for sovereignty.

## 3. Selection bias in the X research

- **Priors shaped the queries.** Tags were x402, 8004, nostr, l402, teehee, confinf, phala, miller and hermes, so the camps found mirror the queries. About 38% of the 728 stored posts match crypto keywords.
- **Shallow and popularity-weighted.** About 25 posts per query, sorted by relevance.
- **Recency.** 676 of 728 posts are from Aug–Nov 2026. That window missed the Feb 2026 Conway Automaton wave: "Sovereign AI" in its title, 7.9k stars, a reported 1.3M views on launch day, and the project CNAS itself cites as the turning point. It also missed the Mar 2026 Berkeley "Self-Sovereign Agent" paper. The stored set has 0 posts mentioning Conway or Automaton.
- **Weak sources carried headline claims.** A 13-view post (x402 numbers), a 7-like reply (TEE_HEE), a 150-view post (Phala), a promo account (Truth Terminal) and an aggregator (Block).
- **X only, English only.** No arXiv, LessWrong, Hacker News, GitHub or EU policy sources. Nothing on the Chinese escape incidents CNAS cites.
- **Also missed:** the summer-2026 containment escapes (OpenAI–Hugging Face, Anthropic, Meta, Gemini, UK AISI), which are why the term is threat-coded; Anthropic's Oct 2026 work on self-replication without a datacenter; TEE.fail; the mainstream agent-payment standards (Google AP2, OpenAI/Stripe ACP, Stripe/Tempo MPP; TRM lists four, the research covered x402/L402 only); confidentialinference.net's 0/13 result.

### Hard open problems (questions for a frontier-tech expert)

1. **Backdoor-free key continuity.** Is there a deployable construction (threshold or MPC signing across heterogeneous TEEs: SEV-SNP, TDX, ARM CCA/Nitro) in which an agent's long-term key survives permanent loss of any one vendor, host or minority of custodians? Recovery would need a quorum of attested enclaves and no human quorum, and the recovery policy could only change through that same quorum. What breaks when one vendor suffers a TEE.fail-class physical compromise?
2. **Identity across upgrades.** When an upgrade changes the measurement, how does a third party verify that build N+1 is the legitimate successor of build N, with the same identity and keys, without trusting the developer's signing key? For example: reproducible builds, a transparency log of allowed measurements, and an on-chain timelocked upgrade policy. How do emergency security patches fit without becoming a bypass?
3. **A verifiable halt that isn't a backdoor.** Can a halt capability be (a) publicly declared and attested, (b) able only to stop or freeze, never to read, steer or move funds, (c) provably the only out-of-band control path, and (d) preserved across migration? One design: an in-enclave predicate checked against an on-chain or threshold-signed stop signal.
4. **Attestation without trusting chip vendors or physical security.** What combination of multi-vendor attestation, ZK proofs of inference, or optimistic re-execution makes an agent's execution verifiable when any single vendor's attestation key may be extracted? What's the overhead today for 7B–70B inference, and why do 0 of 13 confidential-inference providers currently pass end-to-end checks?
5. **Fork-free migration of sealed state.** How do you move sealed memory and keys from SEV-SNP to TDX (or between operators) with an attested handoff where no operator or human can read the state in transit, and the source instance is provably destroyed, so the agent can't be cloned and two copies don't share one identity?

### Revised recommendation (rejected)

> **Rejected.** Kept only so the record shows what was proposed and declined. The project does not adopt 'accountable custody', an owner-held halt key, or 'sovereign ≠ unstoppable' as its frame.

Stop defining sovereignty as "nobody can stop it". Define it as **accountable custody**: every power over an agent (read, steer, halt, upgrade, spend, recover) is held by a named party, provably, and the agent can prove the full list. This keeps the privacy and self-custody ambition, answers CNAS with its own distinction (persistent vs sovereign), and makes a declared halt path a required property at every level instead of a weakness.

MVP (2 weeks), replacing spec + Atlas + scorecard:
1. **Essay:** "Sovereign ≠ unstoppable", with the corrected facts above, the accountable-custody definition, and a disclosure of the Enclava interest.
2. **Agent Control Manifest v0:** a small JSON schema listing who can read, halt, upgrade, spend and recover, each row backed by evidence (an attestation quote, a multisig address, a macaroon or grant caveat, a Paperwire-style grant), plus a CLI verifier. This is the confidentialinference.net pattern (automated, objective checks) applied to agents.
3. **One reference agent** that publishes a passing manifest: an enclave-held operating key, an owner-held halt key, L402 spend caps, a capped grant for any real-world action, deployed on two targets including one that isn't Enclava.
4. **Profiles instead of levels:** five axes (privacy, custody, funding, continuity, accountability) shown as a profile, self-assessed and opt-in, with no rankings of named third parties.
5. **A demand gate before building the Atlas:** build it only if outside builders publish manifests. Until then, list only the components the reference agent uses.

Paperwire belongs in the manifest's "act" and "spend" rows as the reference for delegated, capped, revocable authority, not as a sovereignty layer.
